Step 4 · Skip-ahead self-assessment

Before you spend 3 weeks on Karpathy, answer these without looking anything up. Score yourself honestly.

Q1. What's the difference between a torch.Tensor and a numpy.ndarray?
A tensor lives on a device (CPU or GPU), participates in autograd (tracks operations to compute gradients via .backward()), and supports dtype/shape ops that compile to GPU kernels. ndarray is CPU-only, no autograd.
Q2. What does x.detach() do, and when do you use it?
Returns a new tensor sharing storage but cut from the autograd graph — gradients won't flow back through it. Use when caching a value for logging, or when you want to stop gradient flow (e.g., target networks in RL, or stopping grads into a frozen encoder).
Q3. In nn.Module, why is forward() never called directly?
Calling module(x) invokes __call__, which runs registered pre/post hooks (including those used by features like torch.compile, profiler, FSDP) before/after forward. Calling module.forward(x) bypasses all of that.
Q4. Self-attention: why is the dot product divided by √d?
For Q, K with d-dim entries having unit variance, the dot product Q·K has variance d. Softmax over large-magnitude scores becomes nearly one-hot (peaky), and gradients of softmax in saturated regions vanish. Dividing by √d normalizes variance back to 1, keeping softmax in a healthy regime.
Q5. What's the asymptotic memory cost of standard attention in sequence length n?
O(n²) — the attention matrix is n×n. This is the wall that FlashAttention works around (by never materializing the full matrix) and that linear/sparse/sliding-window attention attacks differently.
Q6. What does LayerNorm normalize over, and how is it different from BatchNorm?
LayerNorm normalizes per-token across the feature dimension (independent of batch and sequence position). BatchNorm normalizes per-feature across the batch. BatchNorm breaks for variable-length sequences and small batches; LayerNorm is batch-independent — that's why transformers use it.
Q7. What is the role of a KV cache during autoregressive generation?
During generation, every new token needs to attend to all previous tokens. Naively that recomputes K and V for the whole prefix at each step (O(n²) per step → O(n³) total). The KV cache stores K and V for past tokens so each new token only computes K, V for itself: O(n) per step.
Q8. Pre-norm vs post-norm — which is used in modern transformers and why?
Pre-norm: LayerNorm before the sublayer (attention/MLP), residual outside. Post-norm: sublayer then LayerNorm. Pre-norm trains more stably at depth because the residual stream stays well-conditioned (gradients have a "clean highway"); post-norm needs careful warmup. All modern LLMs use pre-norm.
Q9. Why do we need positional encoding at all? What would happen without it?
Self-attention is permutation-equivariant: shuffle the tokens and you get the shuffled outputs. Without positions, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" are identical inputs. Positional encoding (sinusoidal, learned, or RoPE) injects order information.
Q10. torch.compile — what does it actually do?
It traces your model with TorchDynamo to a graph, then compiles (via Inductor) into fused kernels (Triton on GPU). Typical speedup 1.3-2× on transformer training/inference, more for inference. Costs: first call is slow (compilation), and graph breaks (Python control flow, unsupported ops) reduce speedup.

Concept 1

Tensors and autograd

A tensor is a multi-dimensional array with three things attached: a device (CPU/GPU), a dtype (fp32, bf16, int8...), and an autograd graph link (if requires_grad=True).

Autograd builds a DAG of operations on the fly. When you call loss.backward(), it walks the graph in reverse and accumulates gradients into each leaf tensor's .grad. The optimizer (opt.step()) reads .grad and updates weights. opt.zero_grad() resets accumulators before the next batch — forget it and gradients add up across batches, which is sometimes intentional (gradient accumulation) and sometimes a bug.

x = torch.tensor([2.0], requires_grad=True)
y = x ** 3
y.backward()
print(x.grad)  # 3 * x^2 = 12.0

Mental model from your background: think of autograd like a distributed tracing span tree — every op is a span, .backward() walks it in reverse to propagate gradients.

Self-check: what happens if you call .backward() twice without retain_graph=True?
The graph is freed after the first backward pass to save memory, so the second call raises a RuntimeError. Use retain_graph=True on the first call only if you genuinely need multiple backward passes.
Concept 2

From a neuron to an MLP

A neuron is y = activation(W·x + b). An MLP is a stack of these, alternating linear layers and nonlinearities. Without the nonlinearity, stacking is useless — composition of linear maps is still linear.

The two facts that matter for everything later:

  • Universal approximation: an MLP with enough hidden units can approximate any continuous function on a compact set. (In practice "enough" is huge — depth helps efficiency.)
  • Initialization matters: bad init → vanishing/exploding signals or grads. Standard schemes: Kaiming for ReLU-likes, Xavier/Glorot for tanh, scaled-init for transformers.

Self-check: if you replace ReLU with the identity in a 10-layer MLP, what do you get?
A single linear layer (mathematically equivalent). Depth without nonlinearity is wasted parameters.
Concept 3

Tokenization and embeddings

Models don't see strings. They see integers (token IDs) that index into an embedding table: a learned matrix of shape (vocab_size, d_model). embedding[token_id] returns a vector that becomes the model's first internal representation of that token.

Tokenizers: BPE (GPT-2/3), SentencePiece (T5, LLaMA), tiktoken (GPT-4). They learn frequent subword merges so common words are one token and rare words decompose into pieces. For images, the analog is patches → a linear projection acts like an embedding table for pixels; for video, patches across space and time.

This generalizes: for video diffusion (Phase 4) you'll have spatial patches × temporal positions, but the idea is identical — a discrete index becomes a dense vector that the model can compute on.

Self-check: why don't language models use one-hot vectors directly instead of an embedding table?
One-hots are sparse 50,000-dim vectors — wasteful and unhelpful. The embedding table is exactly the matrix you'd left-multiply a one-hot by; we skip the math and do an index lookup. The dense d_model-dim output also lets similar tokens cluster geometrically (king - man + woman ≈ queen).
Concept 4

Self-attention — the operation that runs the world now

For each token, attention asks: "of all the other tokens in this sequence, which ones should I look at, and how much?" Then it returns a weighted average of their values.

Mechanically, for input X ∈ ℝ^(n×d):

Q = X W_Q    # queries: what am I looking for?
K = X W_K    # keys:    what do I expose to others?
V = X W_V    # values:  what do I contribute if attended to?

scores  = Q @ K.T / sqrt(d_k)       # (n, n) — similarity matrix
weights = softmax(scores, dim=-1)   # rows sum to 1
out     = weights @ V               # (n, d_v)

Why divide by √d_k? If Q and K entries are unit-variance, Q·K has variance d_k. Softmax of large numbers saturates → gradients vanish. Dividing keeps softmax in a useful regime. This is the single most-asked self-attention interview question.

Causal mask for autoregressive models: set scores above the diagonal to -inf before softmax so a token can't attend to its own future. (Bidirectional models like BERT skip this.)

Multi-head: run h attention operations in parallel with smaller d_k = d_model/h, concatenate. Different heads learn different relations (syntax, coreference, semantics) — see Anthropic's circuits work for examples.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

B, T, d = 1, 6, 16
x = torch.randn(B, T, d)

Wq = nn.Linear(d, d, bias=False)
Wk = nn.Linear(d, d, bias=False)
Wv = nn.Linear(d, d, bias=False)

Q, K, V = Wq(x), Wk(x), Wv(x)
scores = Q @ K.transpose(-2, -1) / (d ** 0.5)            # the sqrt(d) divisor
mask = torch.triu(torch.ones(T, T), diagonal=1).bool()
scores = scores.masked_fill(mask, float("-inf"))         # causal mask
attn = F.softmax(scores, dim=-1)
out = attn @ V

print("attn shape:", attn.shape)
print("row sums (should all be 1):", attn.sum(dim=-1)[0])
print("upper triangle is 0 (causal):", attn[0].masked_select(mask).abs().sum().item())
print("out shape: ", out.shape)
Self-check: if you have 8 heads and d_model = 512, what's d_k per head, and how many learned parameters in W_Q total?
d_k = 64 per head. W_Q total is (512, 512) = 262,144 params (since concatenating 8 heads × 64 d_k = 512). Same for W_K, W_V, W_O. So self-attention has ~4 × d_model² params.
Concept 5

The transformer block

One transformer block = one attention sub-layer + one MLP sub-layer, each with a residual connection and LayerNorm.

# Pre-norm formulation (the modern default)
def block(x):
    x = x + attention(layernorm(x))
    x = x + mlp(layernorm(x))
    return x

Residual connections: the x + ... creates a "highway" so gradients can flow back through deep stacks. Without it, depth kills you.

LayerNorm: normalizes each token's feature vector to mean 0, variance 1, then applies a learned scale and shift. Keeps the residual stream from drifting.

MLP: usually Linear(d, 4d) → GELU → Linear(4d, d). The 4× expansion is empirical — gives the model "scratch space" to process per-token.

That's it. A 175B-param GPT-3 is this block stacked 96 times with d_model=12288 and h=96 heads. The architecture is shallow in concepts and deep in compute.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class TransformerBlock(nn.Module):
    """Pre-norm transformer block: norm -> sublayer -> residual."""
    def __init__(self, d, heads, mlp_ratio=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.ln1 = nn.LayerNorm(d)
        self.attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(d, heads, batch_first=True)
        self.ln2 = nn.LayerNorm(d)
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(d, d * mlp_ratio), nn.GELU(),
            nn.Linear(d * mlp_ratio, d),
        )
    def forward(self, x):
        h = self.ln1(x)
        x = x + self.attn(h, h, h, need_weights=False)[0]
        x = x + self.mlp(self.ln2(x))
        return x

blk = TransformerBlock(d=64, heads=4)
print("output:", blk(torch.randn(2, 16, 64)).shape)
print("params:", sum(p.numel() for p in blk.parameters()))
Self-check: what would happen if you removed the residual connections from a 12-layer transformer?
Training would either diverge or stall — gradients can't reach the early layers. This is the same problem ResNet solved for vision in 2015. The residual is structural, not optional.
Concept 6

Pre-norm vs post-norm, and positional encoding

Post-norm (original 2017 paper): x = layernorm(x + sublayer(x)). Trains fine for shallow models, but at depth the residual norm grows and you need warmup + careful init.

Pre-norm (used by GPT-2 onward, every modern LLM): x = x + sublayer(layernorm(x)). The residual stream stays clean, gradients flow cleanly. No warmup needed in the same way.

Positional encoding: attention is permutation-equivariant, so order has to be injected somewhere. Options:

  • Sinusoidal (original): fixed sin/cos of position at multiple frequencies. No parameters, generalizes to longer sequences (sort of).
  • Learned (GPT-2): a learned (max_seq_len, d_model) embedding table. Simple, but doesn't extrapolate past max_seq_len.
  • RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding) (LLaMA, modern default): rotates Q and K vectors by an angle proportional to position. Encodes relative positions implicitly, extrapolates better.
  • ALiBi: bias attention scores by token distance, no learned position info at all.

For Phase 4 (video diffusion) you'll also see 3D positional encodings for (height, width, time) — same idea, three axes.

import torch, math

def sinusoidal_posenc(seq_len, d):
    pos = torch.arange(seq_len).unsqueeze(1).float()
    div = torch.exp(torch.arange(0, d, 2).float() * (-math.log(10000.0) / d))
    pe = torch.zeros(seq_len, d)
    pe[:, 0::2] = torch.sin(pos * div)
    pe[:, 1::2] = torch.cos(pos * div)
    return pe

pe = sinusoidal_posenc(seq_len=10, d=8)
print("posenc shape:", pe.shape)
print("pos 0 (first 3 dims):", pe[0, :3])
print("pos 1 (first 3 dims):", pe[1, :3])     # different — that's the point

# Demo: without posenc, shuffling tokens gives identical attention outputs
import torch.nn as nn
attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(8, 1, batch_first=True)
seq = torch.randn(1, 4, 8)
out_orig = attn(seq, seq, seq, need_weights=False)[0]
perm = seq[:, [3, 0, 2, 1], :]
out_perm = attn(perm, perm, perm, need_weights=False)[0]
print("attention is permutation-equivariant:",
      torch.allclose(out_orig[:, [3,0,2,1], :], out_perm, atol=1e-5))
Self-check: a friend says, "Modern LLMs don't need positional encoding because they use RoPE." Correct them.
RoPE is positional encoding — just applied multiplicatively inside attention (rotating Q, K) instead of additively to the input embeddings. The model still very much encodes position; it does so in a way that makes relative position natural and extrapolates further than learned absolute positions.
Concept 7

The "obvious in hindsight" tricks

Things you'll see in every codebase, that papers gloss over:

  • Weight tying: the input embedding matrix and the output projection (to logits) share weights. Halves the params used by the vocab, regularizes.
  • Bias-free linears: many LLaMA-style models drop bias terms in Linear layers — modest quality bump, slight memory save.
  • RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm: drop the mean subtraction, keep only RMS scaling. Cheaper, no quality loss.
  • SwiGLU in the MLP instead of GELU: (W1·x ⊙ swish(W2·x)) · W3. Gating mechanism, slightly better than plain MLP.
  • Mixed precision: weights and grads in fp32 (or bf16), activations and matmuls in fp16/bf16. ~2× speedup, ~50% memory cut.
  • Gradient accumulation: simulate big batches on small GPUs by summing grads over N micro-batches before optimizer.step().
  • Gradient clipping: nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(params, 1.0). Stops one anomalous batch from blowing up your loss.
  • Learning rate warmup + cosine decay: linearly ramp LR from 0 to peak over the first ~2% of training, then cosine-decay to ~10% of peak. This is the default schedule for nearly every LLM and diffusion model.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

# 1) Weight tying — input embedding and output projection share weights
class TinyLM(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab, d):
        super().__init__()
        self.embed = nn.Embedding(vocab, d)
        self.head = nn.Linear(d, vocab, bias=False)
        self.head.weight = self.embed.weight        # the tie
m = TinyLM(vocab=100, d=32)
print("tied:", m.embed.weight is m.head.weight)     # True

# 2) Grad clipping — call before optimizer.step()
params = [torch.randn(10, requires_grad=True) for _ in range(3)]
for p in params: p.sum().backward()
total = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(params, max_norm=1.0)
print("pre-clip grad norm:", float(total))

# 3) bf16 autocast — fp32 weights, bf16 compute
with torch.autocast(device_type="cpu", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
    out = nn.Linear(8, 8)(torch.randn(2, 8))
    print("dtype inside autocast:", out.dtype)
Self-check: you train a model and loss is healthy for the first 1000 steps, then suddenly spikes to NaN and stays there. Three things to check first.
(1) Gradient clipping — turn it on at 1.0. (2) Learning rate too high after warmup — try halving it. (3) Mixed-precision overflow — check if you're using fp16 (try bf16 instead; bf16 has the same dynamic range as fp32). Also check input data for outliers — a single bad batch can trigger NaN propagation.

Concept 8

The forward process — destroy structure with noise

Take a clean data point x₀ (an image). At each step we add a little Gaussian noise:

x_t = √(1 - β_t) · x_{t-1} + √β_t · ε,    ε ~ N(0, I)

The β_t values (the noise schedule) are small (say 1e-4 to 0.02), and there are T steps (typically 1000). After enough steps, x_T is indistinguishable from pure noise.

The crucial property: thanks to the magic of Gaussians composing, you can jump to any t directly from x₀ without simulating intermediate steps. Define α_t = 1 - β_t and ᾱ_t = ∏_{s≤t} α_s. Then:

x_t = √ᾱ_t · x₀ + √(1 - ᾱ_t) · ε,    ε ~ N(0, I)

This is the closed form of q(x_t | x₀). It's why training is fast: for each example, sample a random t, sample ε, get x_t in one line. No loop over t.

Mental model from your background: think of the forward process as a deterministic encoder from data to noise — except it's a stochastic, fixed (non-learned) one. The model only ever learns the reverse.

The schedule is engineered so this happens by t = T; if it doesn't, the model has to learn to denoise from data that still has signal, which breaks the assumption that we can start sampling from N(0, I).
Concept 9

The reverse process — what the network actually learns

To generate, we want q(x_{t-1} | x_t) — given a noisy image, produce a slightly less noisy one. That distribution is intractable in general. But if we also condition on the original x₀, the reverse becomes a tractable Gaussian:

q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x₀) = N(x_{t-1}; μ̃_t(x_t, x₀), σ̃_t² I)

with closed-form μ̃_t and σ̃_t. The plan: train a network p_θ(x_{t-1} | x_t) to approximate this by predicting the mean (variance is often kept fixed at σ̃_t²).

Three equivalent things the network can predict, all related by algebra:

  • ε-prediction (Ho 2020, the original DDPM choice): the network predicts the noise ε that was added. Once you have ε̂, you can compute x₀ estimate and then μ̃_t.
  • x₀-prediction: directly predict the clean image. Works, but harder to train at high noise levels because the signal-to-noise ratio is terrible.
  • v-prediction (Salimans & Ho, used by SD2 and many video models): predict v = √ᾱ_t · ε - √(1-ᾱ_t) · x₀. Better behaved across the whole noise range — this is what most modern image/video models use.

Pick one parametrization and stick with it; the loss is the same up to a per-timestep weighting.

The network's job is too hard: predict the entire image from noise. ε-prediction is easier because the noise is what's there. v-prediction interpolates between the two and works across the whole range, which is why it's the modern default.
Concept 10

The training objective — derive it once, use it forever

The principled derivation starts from a variational lower bound on log p(x₀):

log p(x₀) ≥ E_q[ log p(x_T) + Σ_t log p_θ(x_{t-1}|x_t)/q(x_{t-1}|x_t,x₀) ]

This decomposes into a sum of KL divergences between two Gaussians per timestep. Both have known means and a fixed variance, so each KL collapses to a squared difference of means. Substitute the ε-parametrization, drop the per-timestep weighting (Ho 2020 found uniform weighting works better empirically), and you get the loss that runs the universe:

L_simple = E_{x₀, t, ε} [ ||ε - ε_θ(x_t, t)||² ]

That's it. The training loop:

x₀  = sample from data
t   = sample from Uniform{1, ..., T}
ε   = sample from N(0, I)
x_t = √ᾱ_t · x₀ + √(1-ᾱ_t) · ε   # forward process
loss = MSE( ε_θ(x_t, t), ε )      # predict the noise

Surprisingly simple given the page of derivation behind it. You should be able to write the training loop in 15 lines from memory.

Concept 11

Sampling — DDPM (stochastic) and DDIM (deterministic, skip-step)

DDPM sampling: walk the chain backwards. For t = T, T-1, ..., 1:

ε̂ = ε_θ(x_t, t)
μ̃ = (1/√α_t)·( x_t - (β_t/√(1-ᾱ_t))·ε̂ )
z  = N(0, I) if t > 1 else 0
x_{t-1} = μ̃ + σ_t · z

1000 steps per sample. Slow but the math is "trust the chain."

DDIM sampling (Song et al. 2021): rederive the reverse process as a deterministic map (no z noise injection), and you find you can skip arbitrarily many steps. The update:

x̂₀ = ( x_t - √(1-ᾱ_t) · ε̂ ) / √ᾱ_t       # implied clean image
x_{t-1} = √ᾱ_{t-1} · x̂₀ + √(1-ᾱ_{t-1}) · ε̂

Pick any subsequence of timesteps you want (say [1000, 900, 800, ..., 100, 0] for 10 steps). Same trained model — different sampler.

The big idea: training and sampling are decoupled. You train once with the DDPM objective, and at inference time you can swap in any solver (DDPM, DDIM, DPM-Solver++, Heun, Euler, etc.). Modern samplers like DPM-Solver++ can produce decent samples in 10-20 steps. This is why distillation (consistency models, rectified flow) can squeeze it down to 1-4 steps.

Concept 12

The score-based view — diffusion is really an SDE

The score function of a distribution is s(x) = ∇_x log p(x) — it points in the direction of higher density. If you had the score, you could sample by following gradient-flow with a bit of noise (Langevin dynamics): x ← x + η·s(x) + √(2η)·ε.

The key insight (Song & Ermon, Yang Song's SDE framework): a network that predicts the noise ε at noise level σ is, up to a scalar, predicting the score of the noised distribution:

s_θ(x_t, t) ≈ -ε_θ(x_t, t) / σ_t

This means DDPM and "score matching" are the same algorithm in different clothes. Better: the whole process can be written as a stochastic differential equation (SDE), and sampling = numerically solving the reverse SDE. Or its equivalent ODE (the "probability flow ODE"), which is what DDIM is doing.

Why this matters: viewing diffusion through the SDE lens unlocks any ODE/SDE solver from numerical analysis as a sampler. Heun's method, Runge-Kutta, exponential integrators — all of these became diffusion samplers overnight. This is the foundation Karras 2022 (EDM) builds on.

Concept 13

Classifier-free guidance — the trick that makes conditioning work

You want a class-conditional or text-conditional model. The naive approach: train ε_θ(x_t, t, c) with the condition c. It works, but samples are underwhelming — not "obviously" the requested class.

Classifier-free guidance (CFG) trains a single model that can do both conditional and unconditional prediction. During training, randomly drop the condition c (replace with a null token ∅) ~10-20% of the time. At inference, combine:

ε_guided = ε_θ(x_t, t, ∅) + w · ( ε_θ(x_t, t, c) - ε_θ(x_t, t, ∅) )

where w is the guidance scale. w=1 recovers the conditional model; w=0 is unconditional; w > 1 exaggerates the difference, pushing samples toward class-typical regions.

Mechanistic intuition: ε_cond - ε_uncond is the direction in which the conditional distribution differs from the unconditional one. Walking further in that direction (w > 1) amplifies class-typical features. It works because diffusion samples are the integral of these noise-prediction signals — small shifts in the vector field compound over the trajectory.

Trade-off: as w increases, samples become more class-typical but less diverse; at extreme values (w > 15) you get saturated, oversharpened, artifacted outputs. Sweet spot for text-to-image is usually w ∈ [3, 8]; for video usually lower (w ∈ [1.5, 4]) because high CFG tends to amplify flicker.

Self-check: a friend says, "CFG is just averaging the conditional and unconditional models with weights." Correct them.
No — that would be (1-w)·ε_uncond + w·ε_cond with w ∈ [0, 1], which is just blending. CFG extrapolates in the direction ε_cond - ε_uncond with w > 1: the formula simplifies to (1-w)·ε_uncond + w·ε_cond, which for w > 1 means negative weight on uncond and >100% weight on cond. It's a push away from the unconditional distribution, not an average.
Concept 14

EDM — the unifying design space

Karras et al. 2022 ("Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models") is the paper that cleans up the diffusion zoo. They argue most of the design choices in DDPM/DDIM/score-based papers are independent axes that can be tuned separately:

  • Network preconditioning: how you scale the network input/output as a function of noise level σ. Done right, the network sees a target with consistent variance across all noise levels.
  • Noise schedule (σ_min, σ_max, distribution): parameterize directly in σ (the noise std), not in t. The "schedule" is just the σ values you visit during sampling.
  • Training noise distribution: don't sample t uniformly; sample log σ ~ N(P_mean, P_std) so you train more on the "interesting" middle range of noise levels.
  • Sampler: 2nd-order Heun method is much better than Euler; this is purely a numerical analysis choice.

The result: same data, same network capacity, much better samples — purely from design space hygiene. Read this paper carefully; the recipe (their "EDM formulation") is what most modern image-diffusion codebases now use under the hood.

sigma_min, sigma_max, sigma_data = 0.002, 80.0, 0.5
P_mean, P_std = -1.2, 1.2

# Training: sample log_sigma ~ N(P_mean, P_std)

print("sigma quartiles (25/50/75):",
      [round(s.item(), 3) for s in sigmas.quantile(torch.tensor([0.25, 0.5, 0.75]))])

    c_skip  = sigma_data ** 2 / (sigma ** 2 + sigma_data ** 2)
    c_out   = sigma * sigma_data / (sigma ** 2 + sigma_data ** 2).sqrt()
    c_in    = 1.0 / (sigma ** 2 + sigma_data ** 2).sqrt()
    c_noise = 0.25 * sigma.log()
    return c_skip, c_out, c_in, c_noise

EDM uses σ_max ≈ 80 (vs DDPM's effective σ_max ≈ 1 after normalization). Concept 15

Flow matching and rectified flow — the modern frontier default

Diffusion's reverse process is curvy: the ODE trajectory from noise to data wiggles, so you need many steps. What if you just learned a straight line from noise to data?

Flow matching (Lipman et al. 2023): instead of denoising, train a vector field v_θ(x_t, t) that transports points along a chosen interpolation between data and noise:

x_t = (1 - t) · x₀ + t · x₁,    x₀ ~ data,  x₁ ~ N(0, I)
v_target = x₁ - x₀                 # the velocity along this straight path
L = E_{x₀, x₁, t} [ || v_θ(x_t, t) - (x₁ - x₀) ||² ]

Sampling = solving dx/dt = v_θ(x, t) from t=1 (noise) to t=0 (data) with any ODE solver. The training is just regression on a velocity. No KL math, no ELBO, no noise schedule debate. Many practitioners say it just "trains better and faster" than DDPM-style objectives.

Rectified flow (Liu et al. 2023): a procedure for taking a learned flow and straightening it further. After 1-2 reflow steps, you can sample in 1-4 steps with quality close to multi-step. This is the math behind SD3, FLUX, and most modern frontier image/video models.

Why this is now the default at frontier labs:

  • Cleaner training signal (regression vs. noise prediction parametrized choices).
  • Trajectories are closer to straight → fewer sampling steps needed.
  • Compatible with all the EDM tricks (preconditioning, log-normal time sampling).
  • It generalizes: any source distribution to any target distribution. Image-to-image and video-to-video become natural.

Concept 16

Noise schedules and the resolution problem

The noise schedule (how β_t or σ_t varies with t) is one of the most-tuned hyperparameters. Common choices:

  • Linear (Ho 2020): β_t linearly from 1e-4 to 0.02. Simple, works for 32x32.
  • Cosine (Nichol & Dhariwal 2021): keeps SNR high longer. Much better for 64x64+.
  • EDM σ-schedule: log-uniform sampling of σ in [σ_min, σ_max], described in concept 14.
  • Shifted schedule (Chen 2023, "On the importance of noise scheduling for diffusion models"): high-resolution images need more noise to disrupt low-frequency structure. Naive schedules under-noise large images, so the model never learns to denoise from "structured noise." Shift the schedule to higher σ for higher resolution.

This last point becomes critical in Phase 4 (video) and Phase 3 (high-res images). A schedule tuned for 64x64 will fail visibly at 512x512 because at t=T the largest spatial scales still carry signal.

    t  = torch.linspace(0, 1, T + 1)

Concept 17

Why latent diffusion was the unlock

Pixel-space diffusion at 512×512×3 is a nightmare: the network must learn to denoise a 786,432-dim signal, attention is O(n²) over a quarter-million tokens, and a single training step needs hundreds of MB of activations. Going to 1024×1024 multiplies the cost by 16×.

Latent diffusion (Rombach et al. 2022, "Stable Diffusion") sidesteps this by training a VAE that compresses images into a small latent space, then running diffusion entirely in latents:

image (3, 512, 512)  --VAE encoder-->  latent (4, 64, 64)    # 48× smaller
diffusion runs on latent space         # the cheap part
latent (4, 64, 64)   --VAE decoder-->  image (3, 512, 512)   # one decode at the end

This is a ~64× compute reduction per training step for 512×512, and the VAE is trained once and frozen. Every modern image and video model uses this trick — even when the paper doesn't lead with it.

Crucially, the VAE is lossy: fine details (especially text in images, tiny faces, complex textures) can be destroyed by the compression. The diffusion model can never recover what the VAE lost. This is why frontier models (SD3, FLUX) shipped bigger VAEs (16-channel latents instead of 4).

Concept 18

The SD-style VAE — the workhorse nobody talks about

The Stable Diffusion VAE is an asymmetric encoder/decoder, ~83M params, that compresses RGB images 8× spatially. Architecture is a standard ResNet-style autoencoder with attention blocks at the bottleneck. The interesting part is the training loss, which is four losses summed:

  • Pixel L1: ||x - x̂||_1. The "are they actually the same image" loss. Necessary but produces blur on its own.
  • LPIPS perceptual loss: pass x and x̂ through a frozen VGG, measure feature distance. This is what humans actually care about — visual similarity, not pixel-exact.
  • KL regularization: D_KL(q(z|x) || N(0, I)) with a tiny weight (~1e-6). Keeps latents from drifting too far from Gaussian — but the weight is small enough that the VAE behaves almost like a regular autoencoder.
  • Adversarial loss (PatchGAN): a small discriminator trained alongside, judging local patches as real/fake. This is what produces sharpness — without it, VAE outputs are smooth and lifeless.

The KL weight is the trick. A "true" VAE has KL weight ~1.0; you can sample z ~ N(0, I) and get plausible outputs. SD's VAE has KL ~1e-6, so latents are not meaningfully Gaussian — you can't sample from the latent prior. But that's fine, because in latent diffusion, the diffusion model samples for you.

A subtle but important detail: the encoded latents have non-unit variance, so SD multiplies them by a scaling factor (0.18215 for SD1.x) before passing to the diffusion model. Forget this scaling and your samples are gibberish.

Concept 19

DiT — the U-Net is dead, long live the transformer

DiT (Peebles & Xie 2022) replaces the U-Net backbone with a plain transformer. The architecture is shockingly simple — pretty much GPT operating on image patches:

1. Patchify the latent: (4, 64, 64) → 1024 tokens of dim d
   via a Conv2d(4 → d, kernel=patch_size, stride=patch_size)
2. Add learned positional embeddings.
3. N standard transformer blocks (pre-norm, self-attn + MLP).
4. Final norm + linear projection back to (patch_size² × out_channels).
5. Un-patchify back to (out_channels, 64, 64).

The clever bit: adaLN-Zero conditioning. Time embeddings and class labels are injected via adaptive LayerNorm. Each block has a small MLP that takes the time + class embedding and produces 6 vectors: scale and shift for the LN before attention, scale and shift for the LN before MLP, and two gating scalars for the residual outputs.

# adaLN-Zero forward (simplified)
shift_attn, scale_attn, gate_attn, shift_mlp, scale_mlp, gate_mlp = mlp(t_emb).chunk(6)
x = x + gate_attn * attn( modulate(layernorm(x), shift_attn, scale_attn) )
x = x + gate_mlp  * mlp ( modulate(layernorm(x), shift_mlp,  scale_mlp ) )

Why "Zero": the gate parameters are initialized to zero, so at init each block is an identity map. The network starts as a no-op and learns to deviate. This makes deep DiTs (28+ blocks) train stably without the elaborate warmup schedules U-Nets need.

Why this matters: DiT scales way better than U-Net. Doubling DiT params reliably improves FID; doubling U-Net params hits diminishing returns. Every frontier image and video model post-2024 is a DiT variant — SD3, FLUX, Sora, Movie Gen, HunyuanVideo, Wan, CogVideoX.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class DiTBlock(nn.Module):
    """adaLN-Zero conditioning: gate scale init to 0 -> block starts as identity."""
    def __init__(self, d, heads, mlp_ratio=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.ln1  = nn.LayerNorm(d, elementwise_affine=False)
        self.attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(d, heads, batch_first=True)
        self.ln2  = nn.LayerNorm(d, elementwise_affine=False)
        self.mlp  = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, d * mlp_ratio), nn.GELU(),
                                  nn.Linear(d * mlp_ratio, d))
        # 6 modulation vectors per block: (shift, scale, gate) for attn and mlp
        self.cond = nn.Sequential(nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(d, 6 * d))
        nn.init.zeros_(self.cond[-1].weight)             # zero-init = identity
        nn.init.zeros_(self.cond[-1].bias)

    def forward(self, x, c):
        s_a, sc_a, g_a, s_m, sc_m, g_m = self.cond(c).chunk(6, dim=-1)
        mod = lambda h, s, sc: h * (1 + sc.unsqueeze(1)) + s.unsqueeze(1)
        h = mod(self.ln1(x), s_a, sc_a)
        x = x + g_a.unsqueeze(1) * self.attn(h, h, h, need_weights=False)[0]
        x = x + g_m.unsqueeze(1) * self.mlp(mod(self.ln2(x), s_m, sc_m))
        return x

blk = DiTBlock(d=384, heads=6)
x = torch.randn(2, 256, 384)           # 256 tokens = 16x16 latent patches
c = torch.randn(2, 384)                # time + class embedding combined
print("output:", blk(x, c).shape)
# Verify identity-at-init: with zero-init cond, output should equal input.
print("identity at init:", torch.allclose(blk(x, c), x, atol=1e-5))

Concept 20

Cross-attention vs in-context (MMDiT) conditioning

How does text get into the model? Two paradigms:

Cross-attention (SD1, SD2, SDXL): image tokens are queries; text tokens are keys/values. Every transformer block has both a self-attention (image-to-image) and a cross-attention (image-to-text). Text is "looked up" by the image.

# Pseudo per block
x = x + self_attn(x, x, x)           # image tokens attend to themselves
x = x + cross_attn(x, text, text)    # image queries, text keys/values
x = x + mlp(x)

MMDiT (SD3, FLUX): text and image tokens live in the same sequence, joint self-attention attends across both. Separate weights per modality but shared QKV space:

# Pseudo per MMDiT block
x_img, x_text = stream_img(x_img), stream_text(x_text)   # separate MLPs
q, k, v = concat([proj_img(x_img), proj_text(x_text)])   # one joint QKV
attn_out = softmax(q @ k.T / sqrt(d)) @ v
# split attn_out back into image and text portions
x_img += attn_out[:n_img]
x_text += attn_out[n_img:]

Why MMDiT is better: text tokens can attend back to image tokens (cross-attention is one-way). Text features evolve through the network instead of being fixed. The joint attention discovers cross-modal interactions naturally. Empirically: better prompt adherence, especially for compositional prompts ("a red cube on top of a blue sphere").

Modern video models follow MMDiT closely — text/image conditioning streams + a video token stream all attend jointly.

Concept 21

Text encoders matter more than you'd guess

Counterintuitive fact: at frontier scale, the text encoder is often the bottleneck on prompt understanding, not the diffusion model itself. A bigger text encoder almost always helps; a bigger diffusion model only sometimes does.

  • CLIP (~63M-Large, ~700M-G): learned via contrastive alignment to images. Good at "what objects/concepts are present" but poor at compositionality, counting, and long prompts. The original SD1/2 text encoder.
  • T5-XXL (~5B): pure language model, much better at parsing structure and long prompts. Used by Imagen, SD3, FLUX, and most video models.
  • UMT5: multilingual T5 variant; used by some Chinese-language video models (Wan).

SD3 and FLUX use multiple encoders concatenated: CLIP-L (semantic alignment), CLIP-G (more capacity), and T5-XXL (long-prompt parsing). The features are projected and either concatenated (SD3) or routed into separate streams (FLUX).

The practical lesson: if your model is bad at prompts, before scaling the diffusion model, swap in a bigger text encoder. It's the cheapest improvement available.

Concept 22

Noise schedules at high resolution — the shift

You met this briefly in concept 16. At Phase 3 resolutions (512×512+) it becomes a load-bearing detail.

The problem: the original DDPM linear schedule was tuned for 32×32. At 512×512 latent → upscaled images, the low-frequency components (overall composition, lighting) require many more noise additions to fully obscure. A naive schedule under-noises high-res images: at the final step, the global structure of the original is still faintly visible.

Solution 1 — shifted schedules (Chen 2023): scale σ values up by a resolution-dependent factor. For latent space 64×64 → 128×128, multiply σ_max by ~√2 or so. Frontier models use carefully tuned shift values.

Solution 2 — logit-normal time sampling (SD3): instead of sampling t ~ Uniform[0, 1] during training, sample t ~ logit_normal(0, 1) — biased toward middle-t values where the loss is most informative.

Solution 3 — direct logSNR parameterization (Kingma & Gao 2023): parameterize the schedule by log signal-to-noise ratio (logSNR) directly. Cleaner to reason about and the natural variable for many derivations.

FLUX uses a shifted rectified-flow schedule. SD3 uses logit-normal time sampling on top of rectified flow. These choices look like minor knobs but visibly improve sample quality on benchmarks.

Concept 23

Modern samplers — beyond DDPM/DDIM

You met DDPM and DDIM in Phase 2. In production, nobody uses either. The samplers actually deployed:

  • DPM-Solver (Lu et al. 2022): exploits the semi-linear structure of the diffusion ODE. 2nd-order: ~25 steps for SD-quality samples. The default in diffusers for years.
  • DPM-Solver++: multistep variant, even better. Works robustly with CFG (which can destabilize naive solvers). 15-20 steps.
  • UniPC (Zhao et al. 2023): predictor-corrector, ~10 steps for SD-quality. Used by SDXL.
  • Heun (2nd-order Runge-Kutta): the EDM-paper recommendation. Used by most flow-matching codebases.
  • Euler: dead simple — x_{t-Δt} = x_t + Δt · v(x_t, t). Surprisingly competitive for flow matching at 25+ steps.

Distillation takes this further: train a student model to mimic an N-step teacher in K steps where K << N.

  • Consistency Models (Song et al. 2023): a student that maps any x_t to x_0 directly. 1-4 step sampling.
  • LCM (Latent Consistency Models): consistency distillation applied to SD-style latent diffusion. 4-step SD samples that are nearly indistinguishable from 50-step.
  • Rectified flow + reflow: as in Phase 2 concept 15, repeated straightening.
  • Hyper-SD, DMD: more recent advances pushing toward 1-step samples.

Practical guidance: train with DDPM/flow-matching objective, sample with DPM-Solver++ or UniPC. Only worry about distillation when your specific application needs real-time generation — it adds complexity and doesn't improve quality, just speed.

Concept 24

VAE quirks every implementer learns the hard way

The VAE is the silent partner of every latent diffusion model. Things that bite people:

  • Scaling factor: SD1/2 latents must be multiplied by 0.18215 before diffusion, divided after. SD3/FLUX have their own constants. Diffusers handles this automatically; from-scratch implementations often miss it and get gray/saturated outputs.
  • Latents are not Gaussian: even with KL regularization. You cannot sample from N(0, I) and decode to get a real image. The diffusion model fills this gap.
  • Channel count matters: SD1/2 use 4 channels, SDXL also 4, SD3 jumped to 16, FLUX uses 16. More channels = more information preserved = sharper outputs but bigger latents = more diffusion compute.
  • Lossy encoding: faces lose fine features, small text becomes garbled, fine textures (hair, fabric) get smoothed. No diffusion training can recover what the VAE destroyed during encoding. This is why text-in-images is a stubborn weakness even in great models.
  • VAE drift: long video generation in latent space sometimes shows slow color/brightness drift because the VAE wasn't designed to be temporally stable. Causal 3D VAEs (Phase 4) help.
  • Don't fine-tune the VAE casually: if you finetune your VAE after training your diffusion model, all your diffusion-model weights are calibrated to the old latent distribution. You'd have to retrain. Frontier labs decide on VAE first and freeze it.

Concept 25

From image DiT to video DiT — just add a time axis

Mechanically, going from image to video DiT is small. Conceptually, three things change:

  • Latent shape: (C, H, W) → (C, T, H, W). Your VAE now compresses temporally too.
  • Patchification: 2D patches (patch_h, patch_w) → 3D spacetime patches (patch_t, patch_h, patch_w). Sora calls these "spacetime patches" and the term stuck.
  • Attention: tokens now range over T × H/p × W/p positions. Token count blows up — a 16-frame 64×64 latent with patch (1, 2, 2) is 16 × 32 × 32 = 16,384 tokens. Long video at 720p latent can hit 100k+ tokens.

Everything else carries over from your Phase 3 DiT: adaLN-Zero conditioning, text encoders (T5/CLIP), rectified flow objective, modern samplers, CFG. The math is identical.

What gets harder: memory (longer sequences), compute (O(n²) attention bites), and data (video datasets are 10-1000× bigger than image datasets). The architecture is easy; the engineering is everything.

Concept 26

3D causal VAEs — the workhorse for video

An image VAE compresses spatially. A 3D VAE compresses spatially and temporally. Common compression ratio: 8× spatial × 4× temporal = 32× total per pixel. A 64-frame 512×512 video → (C, 16, 64, 64) latent — manageable.

The architecture is a standard encoder/decoder with 3D conv blocks (or 2+1D factorized convs — 2D spatial + 1D temporal). Training loss is the same as image VAEs (concept 18): pixel + LPIPS + KL + adversarial. The discriminator is now a 3D PatchGAN.

Why "causal": in the temporal dimension, the encoder uses causal convolutions — frame t can only depend on frames ≤ t. This buys two huge things:

  • Variable-length input: you can encode a 1-frame image or a 64-frame video with the same VAE. The first frame just isn't influenced by later frames.
  • Streaming: a generated video can be decoded chunk-by-chunk as new latent frames are produced, without waiting for the whole sequence.

Modern 3D VAEs in the wild: CogVideoX VAE (clean reference), Mochi VAE, Wan VAE, HunyuanVideo VAE. They're surprisingly similar — frontier labs converged on a recipe. Treat the VAE as something you load pretrained, not something you'd train from scratch unless you have weeks of GPU.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class CausalConv3d(nn.Module):
    """Pad only on the PAST side of the temporal axis."""
    def __init__(self, c_in, c_out, k_t=3, k_h=3, k_w=3):
        super().__init__()
        self.pad = (k_w // 2, k_w // 2, k_h // 2, k_h // 2, k_t - 1, 0)
        self.conv = nn.Conv3d(c_in, c_out, kernel_size=(k_t, k_h, k_w))
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.conv(F.pad(x, self.pad))

conv = CausalConv3d(3, 8)
v = torch.randn(1, 3, 4, 16, 16)
print("output shape:", conv(v).shape)            # same T, H, W

# Verify causality: changing future frames cannot affect past outputs.
v2 = v.clone(); v2[:, :, 2:] = torch.randn_like(v2[:, :, 2:])
out1, out2 = conv(v), conv(v2)
print("past unaffected by future:", torch.allclose(out1[:, :, :2], out2[:, :, :2]))

A non-causal 3D VAE peeks forward in time during encoding, which gives slightly better latent quality. But it (1) cannot encode a single image, (2) cannot be used for streaming generation (the diffusion model would need to know all future latents to decode any single frame correctly), and (3) doesn't match how autoregressive video generation works at inference. The tiny reconstruction quality loss is worth the architectural flexibility. Every frontier video VAE is causal.
Concept 27

Spatiotemporal attention — three flavors

You have T × H × W tokens. Full attention is O((T·H·W)²) — brutal. Three common designs:

1. Full 3D attention: every token attends to every other token. Most expressive, most expensive. Sora and Movie Gen use this, with heavy sequence parallelism. The cost is real but the quality unlock is large — full 3D attention learns long-range temporal dependencies naturally.

2. Factorized space-time attention: alternate spatial attention (within each frame) with temporal attention (within each pixel position across frames). Cheaper: O((H·W)² · T) + O(T² · H·W) instead of O((T·H·W)²). Used by earlier video models (Imagen Video, Make-A-Video). Less expressive for diagonal interactions ("this object moves and changes color").

3. Windowed 3D attention: split tokens into local 3D windows (e.g., 4 frames × 16×16 patches), attend within windows, occasionally shift windows. Swin-style. Used by some efficient video models and LTX-Video.

The frontier (Sora-class) trend is full 3D attention + engineering (FlashAttention, Ulysses, etc.) to make it fit. The "smarter attention" variants matter less when raw compute is available.

Self-check: factorized attention can't represent a token "object that moves across frames and changes color simultaneously." Why?
Factorized attention applies spatial attention (frame-by-frame, no temporal info) then temporal attention (pixel-by-pixel, no spatial info). To represent "object moves AND changes color," the model needs joint reasoning over the spatial and temporal axes in the same operation — a single attention head needs Q over a spatial position at time t to find K at a different spatial position at time t+k. Factorized attention can only approximate this through sequential composition, and it's empirically worse for fast-motion content.
Concept 28

Conditioning modes — T2V, I2V, V2V

Three standard conditioning regimes for video diffusion:

Text-to-video (T2V): same as text-to-image, but you generate a video. Text is conditioned via cross-attention (older) or in-context (MMDiT-style). The main challenge is text describing motion vaguely — "person walking" doesn't specify speed, direction, or gait.

Image-to-video (I2V): first frame is given; generate the next 15-127 frames. Mechanism: encode the input image through the VAE, place its latent at temporal position 0 of the noise tensor, run diffusion only on positions 1..T. Or concatenate the conditioning latent channel-wise (frame 0 latent is repeated and concatenated to noise input). This is the most commercially deployed mode — easier than T2V because the visual content is mostly given.

Video-to-video (V2V): a full input video is given, generate a stylistically-modified one. Used for style transfer, video editing, motion-preserving regeneration. Mechanism: use the input video as a condition (encoded latent, or pose/depth extraction), apply diffusion conditioned on this.

Frontier models train multi-task: a single model handles T2V, I2V, V2V depending on which conditioning is present. Frame masking during training is the trick — randomly mask out some frames, the model learns to generate the missing ones from the context.

Concept 29

Long video generation — the fundamental tension

You train on chunks (typically 16-128 frames at 8-24fps — i.e., 1-10 seconds). At inference, users want minutes. There's no single solution; the field is actively iterating:

  • Autoregressive chunking: generate a chunk, condition the next chunk on the last few frames of the previous one. Simple, but quality degrades over chunks (drift, identity loss).
  • Sliding window with overlap + blending: generate overlapping chunks, blend the overlap regions. Smoother transitions, similar drift issue.
  • Hierarchical generation: first generate keyframes at low temporal resolution, then interpolate. Like image super-resolution but in time. Used by Make-A-Video.
  • Causal training with KV cache: train on long sequences with causal attention, then at inference cache K/V from earlier chunks. Wan 2.2 and some recent models do this — the model effectively becomes autoregressive in time.
  • Long-context training: just train on longer sequences with sequence parallelism. Expensive but cleanest. Sora's "world simulator" framing implies they trained on much longer context than most.

The honest state of the art: 5-10 seconds is solid; 30 seconds is achievable with quality loss; 1 minute+ with consistent characters is still a research problem.

Each chunk's generation conditions on the previous chunk's output, not its training distribution — so generation errors compound. Concept 30

Camera, motion, and identity control

For video to be useful (not just impressive demos), users need control. Three high-value control axes:

  • Camera control: specify a camera trajectory. Common encoding: Plücker rays (6-dim per pixel: ray origin + direction) which are equivariant to camera position. Plücker embeddings are concatenated as extra channels into the input — like depth conditioning in image ControlNet but for cameras. Used by MotionCtrl, CameraCtrl, and most "controllable video" papers.
  • Motion control: specify an object's path. Common: drag points across frames (user clicks a path); the model fills in physically plausible motion. Models like DragNUWA, Tora.
  • Identity preservation: same character across a long video, or across multiple generations. Approaches: reference-image conditioning (à la IP-Adapter), LoRA-based identity tuning, or face-embedding injection. ID-Animator is a clean reference.

Why this matters for hiring: controllability adapters are LoRA-scale finetunes, achievable with $500-3000 of compute on an open base model. They are the most cost-effective way to produce a portfolio piece that demonstrates real understanding without needing frontier compute. See Phase 7 project ideas.

import torch

def plucker_rays(H, W, K, c2w):
    """Compute per-pixel Plücker rays (origin x direction, direction)."""
    # K: (3, 3) intrinsics. c2w: (4, 4) camera-to-world.
    i, j = torch.meshgrid(torch.arange(W), torch.arange(H), indexing="xy")
    pix = torch.stack([(i - K[0, 2]) / K[0, 0],
                       (j - K[1, 2]) / K[1, 1],
                       torch.ones_like(i.float())], dim=-1)            # (H, W, 3)
    dirs = pix @ c2w[:3, :3].T
    dirs = dirs / dirs.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    orig = c2w[:3, 3].expand_as(dirs)
    moment = torch.cross(orig, dirs, dim=-1)                            # rxd
    return torch.cat([moment, dirs], dim=-1)                            # (H, W, 6)

K   = torch.tensor([[600.0, 0, 32], [0, 600.0, 32], [0, 0, 1.0]])
c2w = torch.eye(4); c2w[:3, 3] = torch.tensor([0.5, 0.0, 0.0])           # camera offset
rays = plucker_rays(64, 64, K, c2w)
print("Plücker map:", rays.shape)                                       # (64, 64, 6)
# Stack per-frame -> (T, H, W, 6), then concat to latent channels for ControlNet-style cond.
Self-check: why use Plücker ray embeddings instead of just passing the (3, 3) rotation matrix + (3,) translation per frame?
Plücker rays are per-pixel — they give the model spatially-distributed information about camera geometry. The model can locally compute "given this pixel's ray, what should it see?" from features near that pixel's ray. Passing a global rotation+translation matrix per frame works in theory but requires the model to learn to apply the projection itself — slower to train and weaker generalization. Plücker is a lifted, redundant representation that's friendlier for conv/attention networks.
Concept 31

Sequence parallelism — Ulysses and Ring Attention

A 720p latent at 5 seconds, 24fps, with patch (1, 2, 2) — that's (120/1) × (90/2) × (160/2) ≈ 432k tokens. Even with FlashAttention, the KV tensors don't fit on one GPU. Sequence parallelism shards the sequence dim across GPUs:

  • Ulysses (Microsoft DeepSpeed): shard the sequence dim, then before attention, do an all-to-all communication so each GPU has all tokens for some heads. Compute attention per-head, all-to-all back. Simple and fast, but communication cost grows with head count.
  • Ring attention: each GPU holds a chunk of the sequence. For Q on GPU 0 to attend to K, V on GPU 7, K/V chunks rotate around a "ring" of GPUs while running matmuls overlapped with comms. Memory is constant in sequence length per GPU — you can go to arbitrarily long sequences by adding GPUs. The catch: lots of communication.
  • Hybrid (USP, Hunyuan-style): combine Ulysses (across heads) and Ring (across sequence). Best of both for video-scale workloads.

This is the topic where your distributed systems background actually shines. The mental model is identical to sharding a database — the question is what you partition by and what comm pattern that implies. HunyuanVideo's training code is the cleanest open reference for hybrid sequence parallelism.

GQA shares K/V across multiple Q heads (e.g., 32 query heads grouped to 8 K/V heads). Ulysses shards across the head dim, so with only 8 K/V heads you can only shard across ≤8 GPUs. Workaround: combine with Ring attention along the sequence dim for additional GPU count, or use a sequence parallelism scheme aware of GQA structure (some recent implementations do).
Concept 32

VBench and video evaluation

Image eval converged on FID and CLIPScore. Video eval is messier. VBench (Huang et al. 2023) is the current standard — 16 evaluation dimensions:

  • Per-frame quality: subject consistency, aesthetic quality, imaging quality, background consistency.
  • Temporal quality: temporal flickering, motion smoothness, dynamic degree (does it actually move?).
  • Semantic alignment: object class, multiple objects, human action, color, spatial relationship, scene.
  • Overall style: appearance style, temporal style, overall consistency.

Each dimension uses a specific metric — usually a pretrained classifier or VLM. Results are reported per dimension; there's no single "VBench score" that captures everything.

Where VBench is blind:

  • Long-range identity drift (it measures local consistency only).
  • Physics violations (cars passing through walls, water flowing uphill).
  • Narrative coherence over multiple shots.
  • Fine-grained hand/face anatomy.
  • Audio-visual sync (it doesn't evaluate audio).

This is a portfolio opportunity (see A19): build an eval that probes a specific failure mode VBench misses, benchmark all open models on it, release publicly. Cheap to compute, high signal for recruiters.

Concept 33

Data curation — the invisible 80% of model quality

The cleanest, most-replicated finding in generative model research: data quality dominates model architecture once both are non-trivial. The Movie Gen paper's data section (90 pages, half of it on data) is the most instructive thing you can read on this — go in expecting to learn more than from the model architecture section.

The pipeline most teams converge to:

  1. Sourcing: licensed datasets (Shutterstock, Pond5), web scrape (with copyright vigilance), or specific verticals (movie clips, YouTube). For learning purposes, use WebVid, Panda-70M, or HD-VG.
  2. Scene detection: chop long clips at shot boundaries with PySceneDetect. Sora-grade models train on clean single-shot clips.
  3. Filtering:
    • Aesthetic score (LAION-Aesthetics predictor) — drop ugly clips.
    • Motion score (compute frame-pair MSE or optical flow magnitude) — drop static clips and overly chaotic ones.
    • Text-overlay detection (drop clips with burned-in subtitles, watermarks).
    • NSFW detection (always).
    • Duration filter (drop <2s or >10s for typical training chunks).
  4. Deduplication: perceptual hashing (pHash or SSIM-based). The internet has a lot of duplicates.
  5. Captioning: every clip needs a text caption. Tools: BLIP-2 (fast, low quality), LLaVA-NeXT-Video (good), InternVL or Qwen2.5-VL (best, slower). Many models use multiple captions per clip (short + long, different styles).
  6. Sharding: store as WebDataset (tar shards of clip + caption pairs) for efficient distributed loading.

Building this pipeline is a portfolio piece on its own (A26). It's also the most-rare skill — most ML engineers never touch industrial data engineering.

Concept 34

Joint audio-video generation — the next frontier

Video without audio feels half-finished. Joint generation is a clear next direction; two frontier examples:

  • Veo 3 (Google DeepMind 2024-2025): generates synchronized audio with video, including speech and ambient sound.
  • Movie Gen Audio (Meta 2024): separate audio generation conditioned on video features.

The technical problems:

  • Modality alignment: video is dense temporal (30fps), audio is much denser (44.1kHz). Need joint tokenization or a careful interleaving scheme.
  • Sync: even small misalignment between visuals and audio is jarring to humans (we're hyper-tuned for lip-sync).
  • Data: most public video data has stripped or low-quality audio. Captioning audio separately doubles data work.

For Phase 4, you don't need to ship audio. But understand it exists — it'll come up in interviews and in research direction conversations. Two minutes on Veo 3's blog post is enough familiarity.

import torch
from einops import rearrange

def patchify_3d(video, p_t=1, p_h=2, p_w=2):
    """video: (B, C, T, H, W) -> (B, N, p_t*p_h*p_w*C)"""
    return rearrange(video,
                     "b c (t pt) (h ph) (w pw) -> b (t h w) (pt ph pw c)",
                     pt=p_t, ph=p_h, pw=p_w)

def unpatchify_3d(patches, T, H, W, p_t=1, p_h=2, p_w=2, C=3):
    return rearrange(patches,
                     "b (t h w) (pt ph pw c) -> b c (t pt) (h ph) (w pw)",
                     t=T // p_t, h=H // p_h, w=W // p_w,
                     pt=p_t, ph=p_h, pw=p_w, c=C)

    • 3D positional embedding (from A16).
    • adaLN-Zero conditioning for time + text.
    • Text encoder: T5-small (or T5-base if you have budget).
    • Config: depth=24, hidden=768, heads=12, params ~350M.
  3. Training:
    • Rectified flow objective, logit-normal time sampling.
    • 16-frame clips at 256×256 (latent (4, 4, 64, 64) → 4096 tokens after patch (1, 2, 2)). 8fps.
    • Multi-GPU training with FSDP. Effective batch size 64-128 (gradient accumulation if needed).
    • ~80-150k steps. 3-10 days on 8×H100 spot, or longer on cheaper setups.
    • Save checkpoints every 5k steps. EMA 0.9999.
    • Class-dropout 10% for CFG.
  4. Sampling: Euler / Heun ODE solvers. 25-50 steps. CFG scale 3-7.
  5. Eval: generate 50 clips per UCF-101 category. Compute basic VBench dimensions (subject consistency, motion smoothness, aesthetic). Note where your model fails.

How to read a video-gen paper in 20 minutes

Frontier papers are long (Movie Gen is 90 pages). You'll waste your life reading every word. The skim ladder:

  1. Abstract (1 min): what's the claim?
  2. Figure 1 (1 min): what does the architecture look like? Visual summary.
  3. Conclusion / Limitations (2 min): what do they say didn't work? Often more honest than the body.
  4. Method / Architecture (5-10 min): the meat. Skip equations on first pass; understand the diagram.
  5. Ablations (5 min): the most informative section. What did they vary, what mattered?
  6. Data section (5 min, only for serious papers): often more important than the architecture.

Red flags:

  • No ablations, or "we tried X and it didn't work" without specifics.
  • Cherry-picked samples with no failure cases shown.
  • No quantitative comparison to recent baselines.
  • "Closed-source" — fine, but read more skeptically when there's no code or replication possible.
  • FID/VBench numbers without specifying the eval setup (which subset, which seed, what model checkpoint).

Concept 38

MoE for video — Wan 2.2 and what it means

Mixture-of-experts (MoE): each token is routed to a small subset of "expert" sub-networks (typically 2 of 8 or 2 of 16). Training and inference cost scale with active params, not total. Total params can be huge (e.g., 100B) while inference cost is small (e.g., 25B-equivalent).

Wan 2.2 was the first major open video MoE. The case for MoE in video specifically:

  • Video data is heterogeneous: nature footage, talking heads, animation, sports — different domains benefit from different network specializations.
  • Tokens are abundant — 100k+ tokens per video. The router has plenty of data to learn good specialization.
  • Memory savings on inference are real — you can fit a 100B-equivalent video model on hardware that would only fit 25B dense.

Implementation challenges: load balancing (don't let one expert get all the tokens — auxiliary balancing loss), communication (token routing across GPUs is expensive — expert parallelism), and capacity sizing (when do you add more experts vs grow dense?).

If you want to specialize: read the Mixtral paper (the LLM MoE that everyone copies), then read Wan 2.2's MoE specifics. The mechanics are identical; only what the experts specialize in differs.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class TopKRouter(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, n_experts, k=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.k = k
        self.gate = nn.Linear(d, n_experts, bias=False)
    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (B, T, d)
        logits = self.gate(x)                                      # (B, T, n_experts)
        weights_raw, idx = logits.topk(self.k, dim=-1)
        weights = F.softmax(weights_raw, dim=-1)                   # normalize top-k
        return weights, idx

router = TopKRouter(d=64, n_experts=8, k=2)
x = torch.randn(2, 16, 64)
w, idx = router(x)
print("weights:", w.shape, " expert indices:", idx.shape)
print("expert load distribution:", torch.bincount(idx.flatten(), minlength=8).tolist())
# Compute = k/n_experts of dense.  Capacity = full n_experts of param storage.
Self-check: an 8-expert MoE with 2 active experts per token has roughly the same compute as a dense model with 2/8 = 25% of the total params. So why does MoE help?
Compute and capacity are decoupled. The model has 100% of the params available (all experts can hold knowledge), but only 25% are touched per token. Different tokens activate different experts, so the effective capacity utilized across a batch is much higher than 25%. Empirically: MoE matches a dense model with 3-5× the active params, at the cost of more memory (you need to hold all experts) and more communication.
Concept 39

Distillation strategies — closing the 50→4 step gap

You met consistency models and rectified flow reflow in Phase 2. Phase 5 surveys the broader landscape:

  • Consistency Models (Song 2023): student maps any x_t to x_0 directly. 1-4 steps. Loss has stability issues — many follow-ups address this.
  • LCM (Latent Consistency Models): consistency distillation applied to SD-style latent diffusion. Practical, widely used.
  • Rectified Flow + Reflow: straighten trajectories iteratively. The math is clean and it composes with anything flow-matching-based.
  • DMD (Distribution Matching Distillation): match the student's output distribution to the teacher's via a learned discriminator. 1-step quality close to multi-step teacher. DMD2 is the current SOTA recipe for image.
  • Hyper-SD: segmented training, breaks the trajectory into pieces. Strong for fast SDXL.
  • Adversarial Distillation: GAN-style training with the multi-step teacher as the "real" distribution.

The tradeoff is always the same: speed up at the cost of diversity. A 1-step model often produces near-deterministic outputs for a given prompt — great for prototyping, less great for creative exploration.

For video, the story is similar but harder — temporal consistency makes distillation more brittle. Hyper-SD and DMD have video variants in research as of 2025; you'll likely build on these for any real-time video portfolio piece.

Video as world model — the robotics/agent path

A separate hiring pipeline runs through "video models as world simulators": companies that use generative video to train robots or game agents. These labs hire from the same talent pool as Sora/Veo, but their work emphasizes action-conditioned and controllable video.

  • Wayve, 1X, Waymo: autonomous driving / robotics. Generate "what would happen if the agent took action A from state S?" as a video.
  • World Labs (Fei-Fei Li): spatial intelligence / 3D world models.
  • Genie 2 (Google DeepMind): playable game environments from a single image. The agent input becomes a control signal.

What's different from T2V:

  • Conditioning is on actions (continuous controls, button presses) instead of text.
  • Coherence over much longer horizons matters more (you need 30-second consistent worlds, not 5-second clips).
  • Physics matters — a falling object that disappears is fine for art, fatal for robotics training.
  • Determinism / reproducibility — for agent training, the same (state, action) should produce a reproducible outcome.

If you're more excited about robots/agents than art, this is a real adjacent path. Same diffusion skills, different application domain. Worth knowing about even if not your primary focus.

Concept 41

FSDP — sharding params, grads, optimizer state

Vanilla data-parallel (DDP) replicates the model on every GPU; only gradients are synced. Fine for small models, but a 7B model in fp32 takes 28GB just for params — won't fit on most consumer cards, won't fit alongside activations + optimizer state even on an A100.

FSDP (Fully Sharded Data Parallel): shard the params, gradients, and optimizer state across GPUs. Each GPU only holds 1/N of each. When a layer is about to run, FSDP all-gathers its params to all GPUs, runs the forward, frees the params back to sharded state. Same for backward.

from torch.distributed.fsdp import FullyShardedDataParallel as FSDP
from torch.distributed.fsdp.wrap import transformer_auto_wrap_policy

wrap_policy = functools.partial(
    transformer_auto_wrap_policy,
    transformer_layer_cls={DiTBlock},   # wrap one block at a time
)
model = FSDP(model, auto_wrap_policy=wrap_policy, mixed_precision=...)

FSDP1 vs FSDP2: FSDP2 (PyTorch 2.4+) has better composability (with tensor parallelism, with torch.compile) and simpler internals. Use FSDP2 for new code.

Wrap policy matters: wrap by transformer block, not by every Linear layer. Wrapping too granularly means too many all-gathers and kills throughput.

Activation checkpointing: forward saves only a few "checkpoint" activations; intermediate activations are recomputed during backward. Trades compute (~30% more) for big memory savings. Essential for training big video models.

Concept 42

Sequence parallelism — the video-scale problem

You met Ulysses and Ring Attention in concept 31. Phase 6 is where you'd actually implement them. Two libraries to know:

  • DeepSpeed-Ulysses: Microsoft's reference Ulysses implementation. Drop-in for transformer training with sequence sharding.
  • Ring Attention: original repo by Hao Liu. Several reimplementations exist; the cleanest are in ring-flash-attention.
  • USP (Unified Sequence Parallelism): HunyuanVideo's hybrid scheme combining Ulysses + Ring.

The pattern when adopting these: usually a thin wrapper around FlashAttention that handles the comm. You still call flash_attn(q, k, v) in your model code — the sharding happens in distributed primitives.

Practical heuristic:

  • ≤8 GPUs and ≤32k tokens: vanilla FSDP without sequence parallelism is fine.
  • 8+ GPUs and 32-128k tokens: Ulysses.
  • 16+ GPUs and 128k+ tokens: hybrid Ulysses + Ring.

import torch

def online_softmax_step(Q, K_chunk, V_chunk, prev):
    """Incrementally fold a (K, V) chunk into a running attention output.
       prev: dict with 'max', 'sum', 'out' or None on first call.
       This is the math FlashAttention and Ring Attention share."""
    d = Q.shape[-1]
    scores = Q @ K_chunk.transpose(-2, -1) / (d ** 0.5)         # (T_q, T_k)
    m = scores.max(dim=-1, keepdim=True).values
    if prev is None:
        prev = {"max": m, "sum": torch.zeros_like(m), "out": torch.zeros(Q.shape[0], V_chunk.shape[-1])}
    m_new = torch.maximum(prev["max"], m)
    p     = torch.exp(scores - m_new)
    out   = prev["out"] * torch.exp(prev["max"] - m_new) + p @ V_chunk
    s_sum = prev["sum"] * torch.exp(prev["max"] - m_new) + p.sum(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    return {"max": m_new, "sum": s_sum, "out": out}

# Demo: stream the attention over 4 chunks of K/V (Ring Attention does this across GPUs)
T_q, T_kv, d = 8, 32, 16
Q = torch.randn(T_q, d)
state = None
for chunk in torch.randn(4, 2, T_kv // 4, d).split(1, dim=0):
    K, V = chunk.squeeze(0)
    state = online_softmax_step(Q, K, V, state)
attn_out = state["out"] / state["sum"]
print("ring-style attention output:", attn_out.shape)
Self-check: why does Ulysses scale with the number of attention heads but Ring Attention doesn't?
Ulysses shards the head dimension — the all-to-all redistributes tokens such that each GPU gets a complete sequence but only some heads. With H heads, you can split across up to H GPUs this way. Ring Attention shards the sequence dim and rotates K/V chunks around a ring; the math doesn't depend on head count, so you can scale to arbitrary GPU count (constant memory per GPU). Combine them when both axes are large.
Concept 43

Inference optimization — bf16, fp8, KV cache, torch.compile

Once trained, models need to run cheaply. Standard playbook:

  • bf16 inference: cast model weights + activations to bf16. ~2× memory savings, ~30-50% speedup. Almost no quality cost — bf16 has fp32's dynamic range, just less precision. This should be your default.
  • fp8 inference: NVIDIA H100+ supports fp8 matmul. Another ~2× speedup over bf16. Quality cost depends on the layer — usually fine for matmuls, sometimes hurts norm layers. Use TransformerEngine or torchao for the kernels.
  • torch.compile: traces the model, fuses ops into Triton kernels. ~1.3-2× speedup for free. Works well for diffusion DiTs because they're regular and graph-friendly. First call is slow; subsequent calls are fast.
  • CUDA graphs: capture the entire forward pass as a CUDA graph, replay it. Eliminates kernel launch overhead. Useful when batch size is small and kernels are short (sampling step inference).
  • Sparse / windowed attention: at inference, you can sometimes truncate attention to local windows without quality loss — especially for high-res images/video where attention naturally falls off.
  • KV cache (autoregressive video): for any model that generates frames autoregressively, cache K and V from previous frames. Same trick as LLM serving.

Graph breaks. Any Python control flow that torch.compile can't trace forces a graph break — the model is split into multiple compiled regions, with overhead between them. Concept 44

Step distillation — make a 50-step model into a 4-step model

You've now seen distillation three times (concepts 23, 39, 44). Phase 6 is when you'd actually implement it. The standard recipe for LCM-style consistency distillation:

  1. Start with a trained multi-step teacher (your A15 or A18 model).
  2. Initialize a student from the teacher's weights.
  3. For each training step:
    • Sample x_0, ε, t1 > t2.
    • Run the teacher one step from t1 → t2: x_{t2} = teacher_step(x_{t1}, t1, t2).
    • Run the student: x̂_0 = student(x_{t1}, t1) and x̂_0' = student(x_{t2}, t2).
    • Loss: || x̂_0 - stop_grad(x̂_0') ||² — the student should map close-by points on the trajectory to the same x_0.
  4. At inference, the student maps any x_t to x_0 in one shot. Multi-step sampling: invert each "one-shot prediction" to get a refined trajectory.

Cost is much lower than training from scratch — typically 5-20% of teacher's training compute. Yields 4-step models that match 25-step teachers.

For Phase 6, distill your own A15 or A18 model. The result is a useful real-time demo and a portfolio piece showing you understand the speedup gap.

Self-check: why do you stop_grad on one of the student's predictions in the consistency loss?
If both predictions had gradient, the network could trivially make them equal by collapsing to a constant (every input maps to the same x̂_0). The stop-grad makes one side a "target" that the other side must match — but the target is not optimized toward the prediction. This is the same pattern as target networks in RL.

def consistency_step(student, teacher, x0, txt, opt, dt=0.05):
    B = x0.shape[0]
    t1 = torch.rand(B, device=x0.device) * 0.9 + 0.1   # (0.1, 1.0]
    t2 = (t1 - dt).clamp(min=0.0)
    x1 = torch.randn_like(x0)
    # Forward (flow-matching x_t)
    x_t1 = (1 - t1.view(-1,1,1,1)) * x0 + t1.view(-1,1,1,1) * x1
    # One teacher Euler step
    with torch.no_grad():
        v_t = teacher(x_t1, t1, txt)
        x_t2 = x_t1 - dt * v_t

    pred_a = student(x_t1, t1, txt)
    with torch.no_grad():
        pred_b = student(x_t2, t2, txt)
    loss = F.mse_loss(pred_a, pred_b)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    return loss.item()

End-to-end video data pipeline

Goal

Build a production-shaped data pipeline that goes from raw video to training-ready WebDataset shards. Process at least 10k clips. This is a portfolio piece in itself — most ML engineers have never built one.

Tasks

  1. Source: pick a corpus. Easiest: a HuggingFace video dataset (e.g., InternVid's public subset). Harder but cooler: scrape a specific niche from a public source you have rights to (CC-licensed nature footage, public domain animation, etc.).
  2. Pipeline stages (concept 45):
    • Scene detect → split into single-shot clips.
    • Duration filter (2-10s).
    • Aesthetic filter (threshold to tune).
    • Motion score filter (drop static + chaos).
    • Text/watermark detection.
    • NSFW filter.
    • Perceptual-hash dedup.
    • Caption each clip (BLIP-2 fast, LLaVA-NeXT-Video for quality). Short + long captions.
    • Shard into WebDataset format.
  3. Tracking: at each stage, log how many clips remained. A 50% reduction at the motion filter is informative. Save the funnel as a chart.
  4. Quality eyeball: at the end, sample 50 random clips + captions. How many do you'd want in your training data? This is the real metric.
  5. Release: open-source the pipeline code (even without the data). Document each filter's parameters. A good pipeline is more impressive than a mediocre model.

from scenedetect import detect, ContentDetector, split_video_ffmpeg

INPUT_DIR  = Path("raw")

def scene_split(video_path):
    scenes = detect(str(video_path), ContentDetector(threshold=27.0))
    out_template = f"{OUTPUT_DIR}/$VIDEO_NAME_Scene-$SCENE_NUMBER.mp4"
    split_video_ffmpeg(str(video_path), scenes, output_file_template=out_template)
    return len(scenes)

def motion_score(clip_path, max_frames=8):
    cap = cv2.VideoCapture(str(clip_path))
    grays = []
    for _ in range(max_frames):
        ok, f = cap.read()
        if not ok: break
        grays.append(cv2.cvtColor(f, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY))
    cap.release()
    if len(grays) < 2: return 0.0
    flows = [cv2.calcOpticalFlowFarneback(grays[i], grays[i+1], None,
                                          0.5, 3, 15, 3, 5, 1.2, 0) for i in range(len(grays)-1)]
    return float(np.mean([np.linalg.norm(f, axis=-1).mean() for f in flows]))

def phash_clip(clip_path):
    cap = cv2.VideoCapture(str(clip_path)); ok, f = cap.read(); cap.release()
    return str(imagehash.phash(Image.fromarray(cv2.cvtColor(f, cv2.COLOR_BGR2RGB))))

Phase 7 — Portfolio Capstone

Goal: one project that demonstrates depth + a clear technical insight + a great writeup. This is the artifact you can discuss for an hour in an interview. Everything in Phases 1-6 has been preparation for this.

The bar — five things your capstone must do

  1. Demonstrates depth in video generation specifically — not a generic ML project, not a wrapper around an API.
  2. Has a clear technical insight or novel result — a new control mechanism, a faster sampling regime, a previously-unmeasured failure mode, a clean reproduction with new ablations. Something a reader would find interesting after the second paragraph.
  3. Is reproducible — public code, public weights (if possible), instructions a stranger could follow.
  4. Has a great writeup — blog post + arXiv preprint if quality warrants. The writeup matters as much as the model. Recruiters can read your writing; they cannot read your training logs.
  5. Could be discussed for an hour in an interview — you understand every design choice and can defend it with empirical evidence.

If your project doesn't pass all five, it's not your capstone yet. Scope down to something you can do well, not up to something that won't be finished.

Project ideas by compute tier

Low compute ($500-3000, fits on consumer GPU rentals)

  • Novel controllability adapter on top of an open base model. Camera control, identity preservation, motion transfer, depth-conditioned, audio-driven facial animation. LoRA-scale finetune on HunyuanVideo or Wan 2.2. Highest hit rate at this tier.
  • Distillation to real-time. Turn an open 50-step model into a 4-step model with consistency distillation or DMD. Ship a real-time demo (Gradio + interactive prompts). Real-time video gen is a hiring magnet.
  • Eval framework exposing a specific failure mode — physics violations, identity drift, hand anatomy, spatial reasoning. Benchmark all open models on it. The framework is the artifact; benchmark numbers are the story.
  • Domain-specific finetune with proper evals. Medical imaging video, robotics simulation, specific animation style. Pair with a domain-specific eval framework — generic FID/VBench won't capture what matters.
  • Multi-frame consistency tool: an inference-time hack that improves long-video identity preservation by reference-image conditioning at each chunk boundary. Cheap, useful.

Medium compute ($3000-15000, multi-GPU node for a week or two)

  • Clean reproduction of a recent paper with novel ablations the authors didn't run. "Authors showed X works at Y scale; we show it generalizes to Z scale and fails at W." Cite carefully; reproduction is a legitimate contribution.
  • A small (~500M-1B) video model trained on a curated dataset with a specific architectural twist (different attention pattern, alternative time embedding, MoE variant). Doesn't need to beat SOTA — needs to show that the twist matters or doesn't.
  • A novel conditioning scheme: pose-conditioned, depth-conditioned, sketch-conditioned, audio-conditioned. Train on top of an existing base. The interesting part is the conditioning, not the base model.
  • Long-video coherence research: a specific technique (retrieval, hierarchical generation, etc.) that demonstrably improves 30+ second generation quality. Measure with a custom long-coherence eval.

High compute ($15K+, only with sponsored credits or grants)

  • A genuinely new training recipe at small scale with strong empirical results. This tier is where you'd compete with academic labs. Only attempt with sponsored compute (grants, friend at a cloud provider, lab access).
  • A new base model architecture at the 500M-1B-token scale, trained on a properly curated dataset. High risk — many such projects don't pan out.

Most successful self-taught engineers ship at the low-compute tier. The "novel adapter on an open base" recipe has the highest hit rate per dollar. Don't pick a medium-compute project unless your low-compute idea is genuinely exhausted or your compute is sponsored.
