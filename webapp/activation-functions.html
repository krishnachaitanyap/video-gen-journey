Why nonlinearities exist

Without them, deep learning isn't deepA linear stack of linear layers collapses into… a single linear layer.

Imagine a neuron is a person making a decision. They get information (numbers) coming in, weigh how important each piece is, add them up — and then have to decide what to do. The activation function is that decision step.

The math behind why this matters is one line:

Layer 1: y₁ = W₁ · x + b₁
Layer 2: y₂ = W₂ · y₁ + b₂ = W₂(W₁x + b₁) + b₂ = (W₂W₁) · x + (W₂b₁ + b₂)
                                                  ↑ just another linear layer

Stack 100 layers of linears without nonlinearities and you still have something mathematically identical to one linear layer. You can only draw straight lines through your data. You cannot recognize a cat. You cannot predict the next word. You cannot do anything interesting.

The activation function breaks the collapse. It bends. It folds. It clips. That bending is what makes deep learning possible. Look at the 3D viewer above — the orange particles are data. Watch how each function reshapes the input.

An activation function takes one number, applies a non-linear shape to it, and returns one number. It's the "decision style" of every neuron in the network. The shape you pick has huge consequences for whether the network can learn.
The eight functions

From light switch to LLaMAEach function explained in five tiers — analogy, problem, visual, formula, advanced.

Step function

1943 Historical · Perceptron
Tier 1 · Layman

The light switch

The simplest decision rule in the world: a light switch. Is the input above zero? Output is 1 (on). Below zero? Output is 0 (off). No gradations, no "maybe," no shades of grey.

Tier 2 · The problem it solves

Modeling a biological neuron

McCulloch and Pitts proposed this in 1943, decades before backpropagation. Biological neurons fire in discrete spikes — either they fire or they don't. The step function was the natural mathematical model. The 1957 Perceptron (Rosenblatt) used this activation and became the first machine-learning model trained on real data.

Tier 3 · Visual intuition

A perfect cliff at zero

Imagine a flat plane at height 0 stretching to the left, then at exactly x=0 a vertical wall jumping straight up to height 1, then continuing flat at height 1 forever to the right. No curve, just two horizontal lines connected by a vertical jump.

Tier 4 · Mathematical

f(x) = 1 if x ≥ 0 else 0

Also known as the Heaviside step function. Piecewise constant — equals 1 for all non-negative inputs and 0 for all negative inputs.

Tier 5 · Advanced

Why we don't use it: zero derivative

The derivative is 0 everywhere except at x=0, where it's technically undefined. For gradient descent, this is catastrophic — if your gradient is 0, there's no information about which direction to update weights. The step function is a dead end for learning by backpropagation.

The Perceptron got around this by training with a non-gradient-based rule. But once backprop became the standard, step functions disappeared from the lineup.

✓ Pros

  • Conceptually simple — yes or no
  • Computationally trivial (one comparison)
  • Biologically plausible

✗ Cons

  • Zero gradient everywhere — fatal for backprop
  • Not differentiable at x=0
  • Loses all magnitude information

Sigmoid

1986 Binary classification outputs
Tier 1 · Layman

"How confident are you?" — 0% to 100%

Instead of a hard yes or no, sigmoid gives you a confidence percentage. Big positive number going in? "I'm very confident, like 99%." Big negative? "Very unlikely, 1%." Right around zero? "Honestly, 50/50." It's the soft version of the light switch — a dimmer that smoothly transitions from off to on.

Tier 2 · The problem it solves

Making learning differentiable

The step function couldn't be trained with gradient descent because its derivative was zero everywhere. Sigmoid solved that by being a smooth S-curve — at every point you can compute "if I nudge x a little, how much does the output change?" That tiny piece of information is what lets gradient descent work.

It also has a beautiful property: output is always between 0 and 1, so it can be interpreted as a probability. For decades (1980s–2010), sigmoid was the default activation in neural networks.

Tier 3 · Visual intuition

An S-curve hugged between 0 and 1

The curve is dead flat near 0 on the left side (large negative inputs), shoots up sharply through the middle (around x=0, output is 0.5), then flattens out again near 1 on the right side. The steepest part of the slope is right at x=0.

Tier 4 · Mathematical

σ(x) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ˣ)

σ(x) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ˣ)            # standard form
     = eˣ / (1 + eˣ)            # equivalent (multiply top and bottom by eˣ)
     = (1 + tanh(x/2)) / 2      # related to tanh by a shift+scale

σ(0) = 0.5. As x → +∞, σ(x) → 1. As x → −∞, σ(x) → 0. Always strictly between 0 and 1.

Tier 5 · Advanced

σ'(x) = σ(x) · (1 − σ(x)) — and the vanishing gradient

Beautiful closed form, but here's the killer: maximum value of this derivative is exactly 0.25, occurring at x=0.

That means every sigmoid layer multiplies your gradient by at most 0.25. Stack 10 sigmoid layers and your gradient becomes (0.25)¹⁰ ≈ 1e-6. The deepest layers in a sigmoid network barely learn anything — gradient has vanished by the time it gets to them. This is the vanishing gradient problem in one sentence.

Sigmoid is still used today, but only at output layers (binary classification) — never as a hidden-layer activation in deep networks.

✓ Pros

  • Smooth, fully differentiable
  • Output ∈ (0, 1) — usable as probability
  • Mathematically elegant derivative

✗ Cons

  • Vanishing gradients — max derivative is just 0.25
  • Not zero-centered — outputs always positive
  • Saturates: huge inputs give nearly identical outputs
  • Exponential is more expensive than ReLU

Tanh

1991 RNNs · LSTMs · GRUs
Tier 1 · Layman

Sigmoid, but it can also say "no"

Sigmoid only outputs positive numbers (between 0 and 1). Tanh is the same S-shape, but centered around zero — so it can say "negative" or "positive" with equal force. Range: −1 to +1.

Tier 2 · The problem it solves

"Zero-centered" matters for training

Because sigmoid outputs are always positive, gradients flowing back through a layer of sigmoid neurons are all in the same direction — forcing zig-zag updates that slow training. Tanh outputs can be either sign, so gradients are zero-centered too. Training tends to be a bit faster, and it was the standard choice for RNNs before ReLU dominance.

Tier 3 · Visual intuition

S-shape, symmetric around (0, 0)

The curve passes through the origin. Far to the left it asymptotes to −1; far to the right it asymptotes to +1. The steepest slope is at x=0, just like sigmoid.

Tier 4 · Mathematical

tanh(x) = (eˣ − e⁻ˣ) / (eˣ + e⁻ˣ)

tanh(x) = 2·σ(2x) − 1           # tanh is literally a rescaled sigmoid
        = sinh(x) / cosh(x)     # ratio of hyperbolic sin/cos

tanh(0) = 0. tanh(±∞) = ±1.

Tier 5 · Advanced

tanh'(x) = 1 − tanh²(x)

Maximum derivative is 1.0 at x=0 — four times better than sigmoid's 0.25. So vanishing gradients are less severe than sigmoid, but still a problem in deep nets: tanh still saturates for large |x|.

Tanh was the default in RNNs (LSTM, GRU) and is still used inside their gating mechanisms. For feedforward layers, ReLU and its variants have replaced it.

✓ Pros

  • Zero-centered output (range −1 to 1)
  • Better max gradient than sigmoid (1.0 vs 0.25)
  • Smooth and differentiable everywhere

✗ Cons

  • Still saturates at extreme inputs
  • Still suffers vanishing gradient (just less than sigmoid)
  • Exponential is expensive vs ReLU

ReLU

2010 CNNs · ResNet · the deep-learning breakthrough
Tier 1 · Layman

The one-way valve

Imagine a one-way water valve. Water flowing in the positive direction? Passes through freely, unchanged. Water trying to flow backward (negative)? Blocked completely.

ReLU does this for numbers. Positive inputs pass through as-is. Negative inputs become exactly zero. That's the whole activation function.

Tier 2 · The problem it solves

It made deep networks actually train

For 20+ years, deep neural networks "couldn't work" — they'd stop improving past 4-5 layers because of the vanishing gradient problem (sigmoid and tanh both saturate). In 2010, researchers showed that ReLU's gradient is exactly 1 for positive inputs, so it doesn't shrink as you stack layers.

This single change — replacing sigmoid with ReLU — is what made AlexNet (2012) possible, kicking off the deep learning revolution.

Tier 3 · Visual intuition

Two straight lines meeting at the origin

To the left of zero: a flat floor at height 0. To the right of zero: a 45-degree ramp going up at slope 1. They meet at a sharp corner at the origin.

In the 3D viewer with ReLU selected, watch how all particles to the left of zero get squished onto the floor (y=0), while particles to the right ride up the ramp unchanged.

Tier 4 · Mathematical

f(x) = max(0, x)

One comparison. No exponentials, no division. ReLU is also the cheapest activation to compute — a single conditional.

def relu(x):
    return max(0, x)
# or for tensors:
def relu(x):
    return torch.clamp(x, min=0)
Tier 5 · Advanced

f'(x) = 1 if x > 0 else 0 — and the dying ReLU problem

The derivative is a step function — 1 for positive, 0 for negative.

The "dying ReLU" problem: if a neuron's pre-activation drifts permanently negative (e.g., from one bad gradient update), its gradient is 0 forever. The neuron stops learning. In some networks, 20-40% of ReLU neurons can be "dead" by the end of training.

Symptoms: whole channels of activations frozen at 0 across all batches. Fix: Leaky ReLU, ELU, GELU, or careful initialization (Kaiming was designed for ReLU).

✓ Pros

  • Cheapest activation possible — one comparison
  • No vanishing gradient for positive inputs
  • Sparse activations (many neurons output exactly 0)
  • Single-handedly enabled deep learning

✗ Cons

  • Dying ReLU — dead neurons never recover
  • Not zero-centered (outputs ≥ 0)
  • Non-differentiable at x=0 (small practical issue)
  • Unbounded above (exploding activations possible)

Leaky ReLU

2013 GANs · small CNNs
Tier 1 · Layman

The one-way valve, but a little water seeps through

ReLU was a perfect valve — blocks negative completely. Leaky ReLU is a slightly imperfect valve: most of the time it blocks negatives, but a tiny amount still leaks through. Specifically, negative inputs get multiplied by 0.01 instead of being killed.

Tier 2 · The problem it solves

Fixes "dying ReLU"

The problem with vanilla ReLU is that dead neurons stay dead — once a neuron's pre-activation is negative, its gradient is 0, and it can't recover. Leaky ReLU has a small but non-zero gradient (typically α=0.01) for negative inputs, so dead neurons can still receive a learning signal.

Tier 3 · Visual intuition

Two lines, but the left one has a gentle upward slope

Same as ReLU on the positive side. On the negative side, instead of being flat at zero, the line slopes upward very gently (slope 0.01 instead of 0). Visually almost indistinguishable from ReLU unless you zoom way in.

Tier 4 · Mathematical

f(x) = max(α·x, x), with α ≈ 0.01

f(x) = x          if x > 0
     = α·x        if x ≤ 0      (α typically 0.01)

Variants:

  • PReLU: α is learned per channel
  • RReLU: α is sampled randomly during training (regularization)
Tier 5 · Advanced

f'(x) = 1 if x > 0 else α

No dead neurons. The empirical gain over ReLU is small — often under 1% improvement on standard benchmarks. With modern architectures (batch norm, skip connections), dying ReLU is less of a concern. But on small networks without batch norm, Leaky ReLU is a cheap insurance policy.

Worth noting: GAN discriminators often use Leaky ReLU with α=0.2 (much larger leak), which works well empirically.

✓ Pros

  • Eliminates dying ReLU problem
  • Still very cheap (one comparison + multiply)
  • Non-zero gradient everywhere

✗ Cons

  • α is an extra hyperparameter to set
  • Empirical gain over ReLU is small (~0.5–1%)
  • Still not zero-centered

GELU

2016 BERT · GPT · ViT · DiT — the transformer default
Tier 1 · Layman

ReLU's smoother, smarter cousin

ReLU has a hard corner at zero — abruptly switching from "pass through" to "completely block." GELU smooths that corner. Instead of a sharp cliff, it's a gentle curve. Small negative values aren't completely killed; they're just heavily reduced. Most of the same benefits, none of the dying-neuron sharpness.

Tier 2 · The problem it solves

Better gradients for transformers

The sharp corner of ReLU creates discontinuous gradients (1 vs 0 with no transition). Empirically, transformers (BERT, GPT, LLaMA, ViT, DiT) train better with smooth activations. GELU is the dominant choice.

It also has a probabilistic interpretation: GELU is essentially "multiply your input by the probability that the input is positive under a standard normal distribution" — a kind of stochastic-but-deterministic gating.

Tier 3 · Visual intuition

Like ReLU, but the corner is softened into a curve

For large positive x, GELU looks just like a 45-degree line (same as ReLU). For large negative x, GELU is essentially zero. The interesting part is the middle: a smooth S-shaped curve that briefly dips below zero around x ≈ −1 before approaching zero asymptotically.

That tiny dip — outputs going slightly negative for some negative inputs — is a key difference from ReLU.

Tier 4 · Mathematical

f(x) = x · Φ(x)

Where Φ is the CDF of the standard normal distribution. In plain English: f(x) is the input x multiplied by "the probability that a random standard-normal sample is less than x."

Practical implementations use a tanh-based approximation that's faster:

GELU(x) ≈ x · 0.5 · (1 + tanh(√(2/π) · (x + 0.044715·x³)))
        ≈ x · σ(1.702·x)                  # cheaper sigmoid approximation
Tier 5 · Advanced

Why GELU dominates in transformers

Smooth derivative, no discontinuities. Combined with the smooth curve:

  • No dead neurons: negative inputs still produce small non-zero gradients
  • Smoother loss landscape: better optimization
  • Self-gating interpretation: f(x) = x · Φ(x) is like ReLU but with a probabilistic gate

GELU is the default in BERT, GPT-2/3, LLaMA-family base architectures, ViT, DiT. When in doubt for a transformer-style model, use GELU.

✓ Pros

  • Smooth everywhere — no dead neurons
  • Empirically beats ReLU on language tasks
  • Probabilistic interpretation
  • The de-facto choice for transformers

✗ Cons

  • ~2-3× more expensive than ReLU
  • Formula is more complex (multiple variants)
  • Marginal gain on CNN-style tasks

SiLU / Swish

2017 LLaMA · Stable Diffusion · modern video gen
Tier 1 · Layman

"x times its own confidence"

SiLU (also called Swish) is the input multiplied by its own sigmoid. "Look at the input, decide how confident you are that it's positive (using sigmoid), then output the input scaled by that confidence." Big positive → high confidence → passes through. Big negative → low confidence → near zero.

Tier 2 · The problem it solves

A simpler GELU that works just as well

Discovered via neural architecture search by Google Brain in 2017 (originally "Swish"; later renamed SiLU for "Sigmoid Linear Unit"). Performs comparably to GELU but is cheaper to compute. Used heavily in modern open-source LLMs (LLaMA family), Stable Diffusion's U-Net, and recent video DiTs.

Tier 3 · Visual intuition

Looks like GELU, with a slightly deeper dip

For large positive x, behaves like a 45-degree line. For large negative x, approaches zero. Between, a smooth curve with a small dip below zero around x ≈ −1.3 (global minimum at about −0.28).

Tier 4 · Mathematical

f(x) = x · σ(x) = x / (1 + e⁻ˣ)

That's it. Just sigmoid multiplied by the input. Notice it's strictly cheaper than GELU's tanh approximation: one exponential vs three multiplications and a tanh.

A parametric version, Swish-β, has f(x) = x · σ(β·x) where β is learnable. β=1 gives SiLU; β=0 gives linear; large β approaches ReLU.

Tier 5 · Advanced

f'(x) = σ(x) + x · σ(x) · (1 − σ(x))

The "self-gating" property — input scaled by its own sigmoid — empirically gives smoother gradient flow than ReLU. In transformers like LLaMA, SiLU is used inside the SwiGLU MLP variant: SwiGLU(x) = SiLU(W₁x) ⊙ (W₂x) — a gated linear unit with SiLU as the gate.

Bottom line: SiLU is "GELU but cheaper, with nearly identical performance." When you're building modern LLMs or image generators, this is the standard choice.

✓ Pros

  • Cheaper than GELU (single exp vs three muls + tanh)
  • Performs comparably to GELU on most tasks
  • Smooth, no dead neurons
  • Used in LLaMA, Stable Diffusion, modern ViT

✗ Cons

  • Slightly more expensive than ReLU
  • Marginal gain over GELU
  • Less interpretable than ReLU's "valve" mental model

Softmax

classic Multi-class output · last layer only
Tier 1 · Layman

"Force these scores into percentages that sum to 100%"

Different from the others — Softmax takes a whole vector of scores and turns them into probabilities. If a network outputs scores [3.0, 1.0, 0.5] for three classes ("cat", "dog", "fish"), softmax converts them into something like [0.78, 0.13, 0.09] — three numbers that sum to 1 and read as "78% cat, 13% dog, 9% fish."

Tier 2 · The problem it solves

Multi-class probabilities at the output

For a classifier with K classes, you need K outputs that are non-negative, sum to 1, and reflect relative confidence. Softmax does this. Exponentiating each score makes them positive; dividing by the sum forces them to sum to 1.

Tier 3 · Visual intuition · interactive

Three sliders → three bars

Drag the sliders below. Watch how softmax converts raw scores into probabilities. When scores are close, probabilities are similar. When one score dominates, softmax becomes almost one-hot.

Tier 4 · Mathematical

softmax(x)ᵢ = eˣⁱ / Σⱼ eˣʲ

For a vector x = [x₁, x₂, ..., xₖ]: exponentiate each, sum them all, divide each by the sum. Always sums to 1. Always positive. The output is a valid probability distribution.

Tier 5 · Advanced

Numerical stability + cross-entropy

Stability trick: subtract the max before exponentiating to avoid overflow.

m = max(x)
softmax(x)ᵢ = e^(xᵢ − m) / Σⱼ e^(xⱼ − m)

Mathematically identical (m's cancel), but now the largest exponent is e⁰ = 1.

The cross-entropy partnership: combined with CE loss, the gradient simplifies to (predicted_probs − one_hot_target). That's why nn.CrossEntropyLoss in PyTorch expects raw logits — it computes softmax + log + CE in one stable fused operation.

Common bug: applying softmax in your model AND using CrossEntropyLoss → double softmax → silently wrong training. Always pass raw logits.

✓ Pros

  • Produces a valid probability distribution
  • Differentiable everywhere
  • Pairs beautifully with cross-entropy loss
  • De-facto choice for multi-class output

✗ Cons

  • Expensive at large vocab sizes (LLMs)
  • Needs max-subtraction for stability
  • Easy to misuse (double-softmax bug)
  • Output activation only — not for hidden layers
